# Traditional Land Use Color Schemes

This file transcribes the survey tables in Sanjay Jeer and Barry Bain, *Traditional Color Coding for Land Uses*, American Planning Association, December 13, 1997 (draft), published as [gismanual.com/style/ColorConventions.pdf](https://www.gismanual.com/style/ColorConventions.pdf). Practitioner notes from a 2002 [Cyburbia forum thread](https://cyburbia.org/forums/threads/question-about-standardized-land-use-code-colors.5065/) follow the tables.

Colors are given as **Prismacolor pencil numbers and names**, the medium these schemes were drawn in. When you reproduce one on screen, match the pencil's hue and lightness by eye, then record the hex you choose. See the [mapping to screen colors](#mapping-pencil-colors-to-screen-colors).

## The schemes surveyed

| Scheme | Source | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| De Chiara (Scheme 1, Scheme 2, Simplified) | Joseph De Chiara and Pratt Institute, *Planning Design Criteria*, 1969 | Three variants |
| North Carolina | American Institute of Planners, North Carolina Section, *A Proposal for a Standardized Land Use Classification System*, 1958 | One of the earliest complete schemes. Many later schemes copied it, and it is "the standard taught in planning schools." |
| Denver | Inter-County Regional Planning Commission, *Land-Use Classification Manual*, Denver, 1965 | Similar to North Carolina, "with some important variations" |
| Anderson | Larz T. Anderson, *Guidelines for Preparing Urban Plans*, Planners Press, 1995 | Coloring guidelines and do's and don'ts. No column in Table 1. |

APA also looked at plan maps from Aurora, Colorado (1989), Sioux City, Iowa (1993), Barnstable County, Massachusetts (Cape Cod Commission, 1991), and Winter Park, Florida (1991).

APA's shorter list of common renderings: yellows for single-family and townhouses, browns for multifamily and high-rise, reds for retail and commercial, purples for industrial, blues for institutional and public facilities, greens for recreation, and grays for industrial utilities.

## Findings by category (APA survey)

- **Residential:** "fairly consistent in using shades of yellows and browns for higher densities, multi-family developments, and high rise residential areas." Mobile homes get other colors: purple in De Chiara, lavender in Denver.
- **Commercial:** "generally colored in shades of orange to red. The denser the commercial, the redder the color." Some maps use pink for tourist commercial areas.
- **Mixed use:** purple shades for commercial and residential mixes (Aurora, Denver), for residential with limited office and neighborhood commercial (Sioux City), and for industrial with limited office and retail (Sioux City). Gray shades for office and light industry mixes (Aurora). Orange and red shades for central business districts (Winter Park, Sioux City).
- **Industrial:** "in most cases rendered in grays or shades of purple. The heavier the industry, the darker the gray." Denver and North Carolina sometimes use blues.
- **Institutional:** "mostly blue." Some institutional types, such as schools and health care, appear in greens and grays.
- **Open space:** "typically green or left uncolored." Greens may also show forest and Indian reservations. Where there are several open space categories: dark greens for agriculture, light greens for recreation.
- **Public and governmental lands** (for example, military installations): light shades such as pink, or left blank.
- **Transportation and public utilities:** black is preferred. Facility types may get different grays when gray is not used for anything else.
- **Environmental** (wetlands, resource protection areas, steep slopes): deeper greens and blues.

### Errors in the published table

- **De Chiara parks: "910 True Blue."** Prismacolor 910 is *True Green*, as the Denver column gives it, but the original draws this cell blue. Treat De Chiara parks as ambiguous between green and blue.
- **De Chiara 1 public buildings: "903 Dark Green."** Prismacolor 903 is *True Blue*, and the cell is drawn orange. The entry is probably a typo.
- **De Chiara 2 "946 Hatched Brown"** for tourist and trailer courts is dark brown with a hatch, not a separate pencil.
- Pencil names have changed since these schemes were published. For example, 922 Scarlet Red is now sold as Poppy Red, and 917 Yellow Orange as Sunburst Yellow. Match on the **number**.

## Practitioner notes (Cyburbia, 2002)

These forum posts are opinions, not standards. They show how the convention is actually used in practice.

- The "classic" assignments practitioners cite: yellow for single-family, orange for multifamily, red for commercial, and purple or gray for industrial. One quoted rule of thumb gives blue for manufacturing instead.
- A regional planning commission colored *existing* land use by type alone, and *future* land use with the same colors plus a dot or hatch overlay for density or intensity.
- APA's classification (later LBCS) includes colors. One planner called it "obnoxiously detailed."
- Colored pencil numbers matched "land use planning textbooks" (for example, #433 for multifamily). Consistency with those numbers was the reason to follow them.
- **For** the standard: it makes maps easier to read and easier to compare across communities. Avoid GIS software palettes such as "bountiful harvest."
- **Against** a strict standard: it was set before modern color ranges existed. Alternatives proposed: shades of tan for residential densities, shades of green for park types, lilac or gray for industrial. Several planners disliked saturated canary yellow for residential and preferred a very pale yellow.
- Many agencies simply pick what "looks good on the computer screen and printed out." Check both.
- Classify by function rather than ownership. Government garages colored institutional would be industrial if privately owned.
- Mobile and manufactured home parks may get their own zone and color (one agency uses a yellow-green).
- **Melbourne & Metropolitan Planning Scheme** (Australia, from 1954), a non-US example tied to the colors of aerial photographs:
  - Residential in pink, not red, because red was too strong for large areas. Future residential: pink with thin black cross-hatching.
  - Industrial in brown: light brown for light industry, brown for heavy. Dangerous industry: brown with red dots. Offensive industry: brown with black dots.
  - Commercial in purple. Service commercial: purple and blue diagonal stripes.
  - Parks in green. Proposed parks on private land: green and white 45° stripes. Conservation in olive green.
  - Rivers in medium to light blue. Main roads in magenta red. Secondary roads in orange. Land to be acquired in dark blue.

## Mapping pencil colors to screen colors

Pencil colors vary with paper, pressure, and pencil age, and the names have changed over time. There is no authoritative hex for a Prismacolor number. When reproducing a traditional scheme digitally:

1. Keep the scheme's hue family and its order from light to dark. That is what readers recognize.
2. Use the cartographic palette in [../assets/landuse-palette.csv](../assets/landuse-palette.csv) as the screen equivalent. Its ramps follow these traditional assignments.
3. Record both the pencil number and your chosen hex in the legend notes or the style metadata.

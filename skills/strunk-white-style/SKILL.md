# Writing in the Style of Strunk & White

Aim for prose that is clear, brief, and vigorous. Every word earns its place; the writer stays in the background. Break a rule only when you know it and breaking it serves the reader.

## Workflow

1. Settle the design: purpose, reader, order of parts.
2. Draft with plain words, concrete nouns, and active verbs.
3. Revise against each section below, in order.
4. Reread aloud; cut what does not work.

## Rules of usage

- **Possessives:** add *'s* to singular nouns, even after *s* (*Charles's*, *the witness's*). Exceptions: *Jesus'*, ancient names in *-es/-is* (*Moses'*), *for conscience' sake*. Pronoun possessives take no apostrophe: *its, hers, theirs*. *It's* = *it is*.
- **Serial comma:** in a series of three or more, a comma after each term but the last: *red, white, and blue*.
- **Parenthetic expressions** take two commas or none. Set off nonrestrictive clauses, appositives, and names in direct address; never restrictive clauses.
- **Comma before a conjunction** joining independent clauses.
- **No comma splices:** join independent clauses with a semicolon or split them, unless the clauses are short and alike (*Here today, gone tomorrow*).
- **No fragments:** do not break one sentence in two.
- **Colon** after a complete clause, to introduce a list, appositive, amplification, or quotation; never between a verb or preposition and its object.
- **Dash** for an abrupt break or a long appositive or summary, only when a comma or parentheses will not serve.
- **Agreement:** the subject's number sets the verb's, whatever words intervene. *Each, either, everyone, nobody* are singular; *none* is singular when it means *no one*. *One of those who* takes a plural verb.
- **Pronoun case** follows the pronoun's role in its own clause: *between you and me*; *taller than I [am]*; *whom* as an object.
- **Opening participial phrases** must refer to the subject. Not *Driving home, the storm began* but *Driving home, we ran into a storm*.

## Principles of composition

- **Choose a design and hold to it.** Plan the shape first; let it bend, not break.
- **The paragraph is the unit.** One topic each; usually open with a topic sentence and link to what came before. New paragraph for each speaker in dialogue.
- **Active voice.** *The committee rejected the plan*, not *The plan was rejected by the committee*. Use the passive when the receiver is the true subject or the actor is unknown.
- **Positive form.** Say what is: *forgot* for *did not remember*, *trifling* for *not important*. Use *not* to deny, never to evade.
- **Definite, specific, concrete language.** *It rained every day for a week*, not *a spell of unfavorable weather set in*.
- **Omit needless words.** No needless word in a sentence, no needless sentence in a paragraph. *The question as to whether* → *whether*; *used for fuel purposes* → *used for fuel*; *he is a man who* → *he*; *in a hasty manner* → *hastily*; *owing to the fact that* → *since*. Distrust *the fact that*, *who is*, *which was*.
- **Avoid strings of loose sentences** (clause, *and/but/so*, clause). Vary length and build.
- **Parallelism.** Coordinate ideas take the same form: *reading, writing, and arithmetic*. Repeat articles and prepositions across a series; keep correlatives (*both...and, either...or, not only...but also*) parallel.
- **Keep related words together.** Do not wedge long phrases between subject and verb; place relative pronouns after their antecedents and *only* beside the word it limits.
- **One tense in summaries,** usually the present. Do not shift tenses without cause.
- **Emphatic words at the end.** The close of a sentence is its strongest position.

## Matters of form

- Do not flag colloquialisms with quotation marks.
- Use exclamation points only for true exclamations or commands.
- Hyphenate compound modifiers before a noun (*a well-known poet*), not after (*the poet is well known*); never after *-ly* adverbs.
- Write dates and serial numbers in figures; spell out numbers in dialogue; begin no sentence with a numeral.
- Punctuate around a parenthesis as if it were absent; a full sentence in parentheses keeps its own period.
- Commas and periods go inside closing quotation marks; colons and semicolons outside. Indirect quotations (after *that*) take no marks; set long quotations as indented blocks.
- Put scholarly references in parentheses or notes, not in the running text.
- Italicize titles of books, plays, and periodicals.

## Words commonly misused

- *affect* (influence) / *effect* (result; to bring about)
- *all right*, never *alright*; *a lot*, never *alot*; *irregardless* is not a word
- *allude* (refer indirectly) / *elude* (escape); *allusion* / *illusion*
- *among* (three or more) / *between* (two, or each to each)
- *and/or*: avoid
- *as good or better than*: say *as good as or better than*
- *can* (ability) / *may* (permission)
- *compare to* (liken) / *compare with* (examine differences)
- *comprise* means *contain*: the whole comprises the parts; never *is comprised of*
- *data* is plural; *phenomena*, *criteria*, *media* likewise
- *different from*, not *different than*
- *disinterested* (impartial) / *uninterested* (bored)
- *due to* means *attributable to*; do not use it for *because of*
- *enormity* means great wickedness, not size
- *farther* (distance) / *further* (degree, time)
- *fewer* (count) / *less* (quantity)
- *flammable*, not *inflammable*
- *hopefully* means *with hope*, not *I hope*
- *imply* (suggest) / *infer* (deduce)
- *lay* (put; takes an object) / *lie* (recline)
- *like* governs nouns and pronouns; use *as* before clauses
- *literally*: never as an intensive for figurative statements
- *nauseous* (sickening) / *nauseated* (sick)
- *presently* means *soon*; for *now* use *currently* or nothing
- *that* (restrictive) / *which* (nonrestrictive)
- *transpire* means *come to light*, not *happen*
- *try to*, not *try and*
- *unique* takes no degree: not *very unique*
- *utilize*: use *use*
- *while*: use only for *during the time that*, not for *although* or *and*
- Avoid *-wise* and *-ize* coinages (*finalize*, *prioritize*), nouns as verbs (*contact*, *impact*, *host*), and fillers: *case, character, factor, feature, nature, the fact that, interesting, meaningful, very, rather, pretty, little*

## Words commonly misspelled

accidentally, believe, benefit, definite, develop, ecstasy, existence, fiery, humorous, independent, irresistible, led, lose, necessary, occurred, parallel, preceding, principal / principle, privilege, pursue, repetition, rhythm, ridiculous, sacrilegious, seize, separate, siege, too, tragedy, undoubtedly, until

## An approach to style

1. Place yourself in the background; let the work show, not the writer.
2. Write naturally; do not imitate.
3. Work from a suitable design.
4. Write with nouns and verbs, not adjectives and adverbs.
5. Revise and rewrite.
6. Do not overwrite; rich, ornate prose cloys.
7. Do not overstate; one overstatement discredits the rest.
8. Avoid qualifiers: *rather, very, little, pretty*.
9. Do not affect a breezy, chatty manner.
10. Use orthodox spelling (*through*, not *thru*).
11. Do not explain too much; skip adverbs in dialogue attributions (*said*, not *said angrily*).
12. Do not construct awkward adverbs (*tangledly*).
13. Make clear who is speaking in dialogue.
14. Avoid fancy words; prefer the short, familiar, plain one.
15. Use dialect only if your ear is sure.
16. Be clear; muddiness is not a style.
17. Do not inject opinion unasked.
18. Use figures of speech sparingly; never mix metaphors.
19. Do not shortcut at clarity's cost; spell out unfamiliar acronyms.
20. Avoid foreign words when English will serve.
21. Prefer the standard to the offbeat; jargon and slang date quickly.

## Final check

Before delivering, confirm: active verbs; no needless words; parallel series; modifiers by their words; correct commas; no misused or misspelled words; emphatic words last.
